SAY: That’s where Anchor comes in. Right matches all move together, so they agree with each other. Anchor keeps the biggest group that agrees and drops the rest. Then it fixes the map with the good matches and checks its own work, so it can warn the surgeon if the fix is bad. — IF ASKED: Be precise: Anchor checks matches; it does not find them or read ultrasound images. Only step 1 runs on the quantum computer. Steps 2 and 3 are normal-computer steps, and step 3 (the self-check) works with any method. The next two slides explain step 1, then one slide covers steps 2 and 3. Two matches “agree” when the distance between their two MRI landmarks equals the distance between their two ultrasound points, within a tolerance we fixed from the data before any quantum run. The brain does bend a little, which is why there is a tolerance. The drawing is a simplified example, not patient data. Fix: a thin-plate spline, a smooth stretch that passes exactly through the kept landmark pairs and fades out away from them. Check: leave-one-out. For each kept match, refit without it and measure how far the others predict it should be; large misses colour that area red. The alarm thresholds were tuned on half the patients and graded on the other half (results on slide 11). This is a normal-computer step that works with any method, so don’t pitch the colours as a quantum feature.
