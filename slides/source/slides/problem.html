1 · The problem

The surgeon’s map goes out of date.

Before surgery, the patient gets an MRI: a detailed 3D scan of the brain. During the operation, a navigation system shows the surgeon where their tools are on that MRI.

But once the skull is opened, the brain sags and shifts, by up to 2 cm. Doctors call this brain shift.

Now the map is wrong, so surgeons can leave tumour behind or cut healthy brain. The only full fix today is an MRI machine in the operating room, which costs millions and few hospitals have.

The MRI map. Purple dots are landmarks: spots that can be found again on later scans.

The pre-operative MRI is taken days before surgery; surgical navigation (like a GPS for the surgeon’s tools) lines the patient’s head up with it at the start. The shift is up to 2 cm, about 5 mm on average, from fluid loss, gravity, swelling and removed tissue. Who it affects: brain surgeons, their patients, and hospitals that can’t afford an MRI machine in the operating room (millions of dollars). How much tumour is removed is tied to survival. The 3D brain is a standard model brain for display; all measurements come from real patient data.
